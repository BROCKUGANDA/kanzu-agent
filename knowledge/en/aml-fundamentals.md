# AML/CFT fundamentals for deposit-taking cooperatives

## The risk-based approach

FATF Recommendation 1 requires an institution to identify, assess and understand
its own money-laundering and terrorist-financing risks, and then to allocate
controls in proportion to those risks. For a savings and credit cooperative this
means controls are calibrated to the member base, the products offered, the
delivery channels used and the geographies touched — not copied from a commercial
bank.

A risk-based approach explicitly permits simplified measures where risk is low.
That matters for financial inclusion: a blanket requirement for extensive
documentation on every small saver pushes low-risk members out of the formal
system and into cash, which increases systemic risk rather than reducing it. The
corollary is that enhanced measures are mandatory where risk is higher.

Risk assessment is a document, not an attitude. It must be written down, approved
by the board, and revisited when the institution changes what it sells or who it
sells to.

## Customer due diligence and tiering

Recommendation 10 requires identifying the member and verifying that identity
using reliable, independent source documents; identifying the beneficial owner
where the member is not a natural person; understanding the intended purpose of
the relationship; and conducting ongoing monitoring.

Tiered CDD is the normal implementation. Lower tiers permit account opening on
reduced documentation but carry correspondingly lower transaction ceilings. The
control only works if the ceiling is actually enforced by the ledger system: an
unverified member transacting at volume is the single most common compliance
failure found in small institutions, and it is trivially detectable in software.

CDD is not a one-off event at onboarding. Where the institution cannot complete or
refresh due diligence, it must consider whether the relationship can continue and
whether a report is warranted.

## Ongoing monitoring and unusual activity

Monitoring compares activity against what is expected for that member. The
reference point is the member's own established pattern and stated purpose, not an
institution-wide average. A market trader banking three hundred thousand shillings
a week is unremarkable; the same figure from a member whose salary account has
never exceeded twenty thousand is not.

Transactions that are unusual are not necessarily suspicious. The obligation is to
examine the background and purpose, record what the examination found, and
escalate only where suspicion survives that examination. Documenting a decision
not to escalate is as important as documenting one to escalate.

## The reporting obligation

Recommendation 20 requires that where an institution suspects, or has reasonable
grounds to suspect, that funds are the proceeds of criminal activity or relate to
terrorist financing, it must promptly report to the financial intelligence unit.
The threshold is suspicion, which is lower than proof and does not require the
institution to identify the predicate offence.

Two rules follow and are frequently mishandled. Reporting is protected: an
institution and its staff acting in good faith are shielded from liability for
disclosure. Reporting is confidential: tipping off the member that a report has
been or may be made is a separate offence in most jurisdictions.

Separately from suspicion-based reporting, many jurisdictions require reports on
cash transactions above a prescribed value regardless of suspicion. That value is
set by national instrument and changes; it must be read from the current
regulation and configured in the institution's own policy, never hard-coded from
memory.

## Record keeping

Recommendation 11 sets a floor of five years for retention of transaction records
and CDD documentation, running from the end of the relationship or the date of the
occasional transaction. National law frequently requires longer, so the operative
retention period must be confirmed against the local instrument.

Records must be sufficient to permit reconstruction of individual transactions and
must be available to competent authorities on request. In practice this means the
identifier, timestamp, amount, currency, channel, counterparty and narrative of
each movement, plus the reasoning behind any escalation decision.

## Governance and the reporting officer

An institution must appoint a compliance officer at management level with
responsibility for the AML/CFT programme, provide ongoing staff training, and
maintain an independent audit function to test the programme. In a small
cooperative these functions are often held by very few people, which makes written
procedure and an immutable audit trail more important, not less.
